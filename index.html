What is CBAM?

The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM, Regulation (EU) 2023/956) puts a carbon price on certain goods imported into the EU: aluminium, iron and steel, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen and electricity. It makes imported goods pay the same carbon price that EU producers already pay under the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), so production does not simply move to countries with weaker climate rules.

Who pays and when

Since 1 January 2026, EU importers owe one CBAM certificate for every tonne of CO2e embedded in the goods. Certificates are bought from February 2027 at a price that follows the EU carbon price, €77.65 on average so far in 2026.

How the charge is worked out

Embedded emissions minus a benchmark. The benchmark deduction shrinks each year as the EU phases out free allowances (97.5% in 2026, 0% by 2034), so the charge rises every year.

Who is exempt

Goods made in Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Switzerland, whose carbon markets are part of or linked to the EU ETS, and importers bringing in less than 50 tonnes a year. The UK starts its own CBAM on 1 January 2027.

Three types of aluminium

Primary aluminium

Made from bauxite, refined into alumina and smelted with large amounts of electricity. Smelting releases CO2 and PFCs from the carbon anodes. It uses the CBAM primary route benchmark.

Recycled aluminium

Scrap that is remelted into new metal. It needs only a small part of the energy of primary aluminium, so its direct emissions are much lower. A plant that makes metal from scrap uses the much lower secondary route benchmark.

Low carbon aluminium

Primary aluminium made with low carbon electricity, such as hydropower, and sometimes newer anode technology. It is still primary route metal, so it uses the primary benchmark. Because CBAM does not count electricity, it only lowers CBAM if its direct emissions are lower and the supplier can prove it.

Only direct emissions count for aluminium (fuel, anodes, PFCs), not electricity. Alumina is not covered.

Why real (verified) values matter. Without verified data from your supplier, CBAM must use the EU default value: one number per country, based on how aluminium is usually made there, plus a mark up of 10% to 30%. It is the same for every tonne, so buying recycled or low carbon aluminium from that supplier does not lower your CBAM at all. With verified data, each type is charged on its real emissions and recycled metal gets its lower benchmark, so a switch to low emission aluminium really does cut the cost.

Where you import into decides the rules

An EU country. EU CBAM applies to metal made outside the EU.

The United Kingdom. UK CBAM applies from 2027 and charges only the difference from any carbon price already paid.

Any other country (for example the US or India). No CBAM on the sheet itself, but metal made or rolled in Europe still carries the EU carbon cost in its price.

The supply chains compared

Imported sheet. The sheet is made in another country and shipped to you. CBAM, if any, is paid by you.

EU made. Every step happens in the EU. No CBAM; the EU carbon price is built into the price.

EU smelted and rolled. Alumina comes from outside the EU (it carries no CBAM); smelting and rolling happen in the EU.

Imported ingot, EU rolled. Ingots are made outside the EU and rolled in the EU; the EU roller pays CBAM on them and passes it on.

Imported ingot, UK rolled. Only for UK buyers: ingots are imported into the UK and rolled there; the UK roller pays UK CBAM from 2027.

CBAM factor, CSCF and default value mark ups

What the mark up means. If your supplier cannot give verified emissions data, you must use the EU's default emission value for that country and product instead. The EU sets these defaults high on purpose, and adds a mark up on top: 10% in 2026, 20% in 2027 and 30% from 2028. It works as a penalty for not having real data, so suppliers with verified data usually cost less in CBAM.

EU ETS benchmarks

For aluminium made in the EU

A benchmark is the number of free allowances the EU gives per tonne produced; emissions above it are paid for. Smelting and anode production have their own product benchmarks. Alumina refining and rolling do not, so their benchmark per tonne is the heat they use multiplied by the heat benchmark.

Product benchmarks

Heat benchmarks

Alumina refining and rolling have no product benchmark of their own. Instead, the EU gives free allowances for the heat they use: 31.2 allowances for every terajoule (TJ) of heat. Enter how much heat each process uses to make one tonne of aluminium; the tool multiplies it by the heat benchmark to get the free allowances per tonne. For example, 0.0212 TJ × 31.2 = 0.66 allowances per tonne for alumina refining.

CBAM benchmarks

For imported ingot and sheet

CBAM sets benchmarks by production route, not by aluminium type: primary route (K) and secondary route (L). There is no separate benchmark for low carbon aluminium.

With EU default values

The route comes from the EU default values table for the country of origin. If the country is listed as primary (K), the primary benchmark is used for everything you buy from it, even recycled metal. If it is listed as secondary (L), the secondary benchmark is used.

With verified supplier data

The route follows how the metal was really made. Recycled aluminium uses the secondary benchmark (L). Primary and low carbon aluminium use the primary benchmark (K).

What does switching to low emission aluminium save?

Recycled and low carbon aluminium carry fewer embedded emissions, so less carbon cost. Set how much of each you plan to buy and compare the cost with business as usual. Your results update as you type.

You only see a CBAM saving with real supplier data. If your supplier has no verified emissions data, CBAM uses the EU default value for the country. That value is the same whatever you buy, so recycled and low carbon aluminium show no CBAM saving on imported metal, even though they really are cleaner. Ask your supplier for verified emissions data and enter it in the CBAM emission factors table to see the saving.

Low carbon aluminium saves less than you might expect. CBAM and the EU ETS only count direct emissions (Scope 1). Low carbon aluminium is cleaner mainly because it uses renewable electricity, which is not counted. So even with real data, most of its benefit does not show up as a lower carbon cost. Recycled aluminium gives the biggest saving.

    How the calculator works

    Every number the tool shows comes from the formulas below and the sources listed. All costs are per tonne of aluminium sheet, worked out year by year.

    Formulas

    1. Weighted emissions = share of primary × its factor + share of low carbon × its factor + share of recycled × its factor.
    2. Free allocation share for each year comes from the EU ETS Directive: 97.5% (2026), 95%, 90%, 77.5%, 51.5%, 39%, 26.5%, 14%, 0% (2034 onward). The CBAM phase in is 100% minus this share.
    3. EU ETS cost built into the price (EU made metal), for each process step: emissions charged = MAX(0, emissions minus benchmark × free allocation share). Smelting uses the aluminium product benchmark and anode production the prebake anode benchmark × anodes used, both on the primary and low carbon share; alumina refining and rolling use heat used × heat benchmark. Alumina refining keeps its full free allocation (free allocation share = 100%), because alumina is not a CBAM good and the phase out only applies to CBAM goods. Cost = emissions charged × EU carbon price.
    4. EU CBAM on imported sheet or ingots, verified data: certificates = sum over types of share × MAX(0, embedded emissions minus CBAM benchmark × free allocation share). Primary and low carbon use the primary route benchmark; recycled uses the secondary route benchmark.
    5. EU CBAM with EU default values: certificates = MAX(0, default × (1 + mark up) minus the benchmark of the route given for that country in the default values file × free allocation share). Example: China sheet is primary route (1.485), Serbia sheet is secondary route (0.148). Mark up 10% (2026), 20% (2027), 30% from 2028. The mix does not change this.
    6. EU carbon price: 2026 uses the published CBAM certificate price; 2027 to 2030 use the Reuters analyst survey of July 2026 unless you type your own; later years continue the analysts' trend of about 8% a year rate. The carbon price check slider scales the whole path.
    7. CBAM cost = certificates × EU certificate price. The phase in is applied once, through the benchmark. Carbon price already paid in the country of origin (for example the UK) is deducted. Importers bringing in less than 50 tonnes a year are exempt.
    8. UK CBAM (importing into the UK, from 1 January 2027, only if your CBAM imports are worth £50,000 or more over 12 months) = direct embedded emissions × UK price (entered in GBP) × (1 minus UK free allowance share), minus the carbon price already paid in the country of origin after free allowances, never below zero. EU default values are used until the UK publishes its own, and the UK free allowance path is an assumption until HM Treasury publishes the rate.
    9. From the UK into the EU: EU CBAM as normal, minus the UK carbon price already paid after UK free allowances, never below zero.
    10. Importing into a country with no CBAM (for example the US or India): no CBAM on the sheet itself. Metal made or rolled in Europe still carries the EU ETS cost in its price, and ingots rolled in the EU still carry the EU CBAM the roller pays.
    11. Imported ingot, UK rolled (UK buyers only) = UK CBAM on the ingots from 2027 (paid by the UK roller) + UK ETS cost of rolling, both built into the price.
    12. Total cost per tonne = direct CBAM + indirect cost built into the price. Total per year = cost per tonne × tonnes bought.
    13. Totals = each year's cost added up, not discounted.
    14. Blended supply = sum of each scenario's cost per tonne × its share of your purchases.
    15. Break even emissions = CBAM benchmark × free allocation share for that year. At or below it, no certificates are due.
    16. Break even premium = cost per tonne if all primary minus cost per tonne if all recycled (or all low carbon), in each scenario.
    17. Emissions avoided = (weighted direct emissions with today's mix minus with your new mix) × tonnes bought. Covered emissions per scenario: A imported sheet; B refining, smelting and rolling; C smelting and rolling; D and E imported ingots plus rolling.

    Limits

    • Emission factors marked "original model value" come from company data and have not been checked against a public source.
    • Benchmarks after 2030 are assumed to stay at today's values. EU carbon prices after 2030 continue the analysts' trend of about 8% a year unless you type your own.
    • The tool shows carbon costs only. Aluminium prices, price premiums for recycled or low carbon metal, transport and duties are not included.
    • Anode production is assumed to lose free allocation on the same phase out as smelting, because anodes are used only to make aluminium. If anode plants keep full free allocation, the EU made cost is slightly lower.
    • EU made emission factors are midpoints of EU plant data from the EU benchmark study (2009), the most recent official split by process step. Plants have likely improved since, so they are on the cautious side.
    • Recycled metal remelted in the EU gets no free allocation in this tool (its fuel benchmark is left out), so its EU ETS cost is slightly overstated.
    • Verified sheet imports use the full chain sheet benchmark (1.485 primary, 0.148 secondary). The official calculation for actual emissions adds the rolling step benchmark to the benchmark of the ingots used, which comes out within about 0.01 t of these values.
    • Results are estimates, not legal or customs advice. Rules and prices last checked 6 October 2026.
